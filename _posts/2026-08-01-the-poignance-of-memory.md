## "You will forget this ever happened within just a few days."

This is a story about two people.

One winter when I was still very young and naive, even more than I am now, I traveled with my parents to Chicago, the suburbs of which was where I lived. There happened to be a glorious sunset on this day, as the great blue clashed spectacularly with purple and orange in one last act of fierce defiance. As I walked into the underground train station during the evening, perhaps holding my mother's hand, I saw a man sitting on the floor. He was homeless, a fact which is so tragically unremarkable that I may not even need to mention it at all. In front of him on the ground there was an oblong, dark object, as black as the asphalt above. Hovering over the man was another individual dressed as a doctor, carrying a bag. My best guess was that he was on his way home from his job at some medical facility. The doctor was imploring the man on the ground, who was unresponsive, to go to the emergency room, that if he didn't get to the hospital, he would die. This elicited no verbal response from the man on the ground, who did not even bother, or perhaps was unable, to move. The doctor gave up pretty quickly; he had a train to catch.

As I got closer, it became more clear what the object on the ground was, or rather, that it was not an object at all. It was the man's bare leg, rotting in matte black from his ankle to his knee. The leg was so swollen that it lacked the contours human anatomy should have given it, grotesquely dry and brittle. For a sheltered, relatively silver-spoon child, such a sight was enough to galvanize me into action. I cannot help but imagine pushing my finger through his limb and feeling it crumble. If I did this, I further imagined I would find some purple, gray liquid, not at all what a human leg should contain. These are but afterthoughts now, as I write with a more lucid perspective.

That night, however, I chose to speak. With what little innocence my younger self possessed, I walked by the man, hypnotized by this rotten limb, not understanding how something so dead could be attached to a living person. I shouted in reproach to my parents to call an ambulance (impossible considering we were in a station in retrospect), to get help, to do something. To my great dismay, they shook their heads vigorously and pulled me along, and with that, pulled my naivete, my childlike tendency to believe in optimism, in anything but the blackness of reality, until I emerged into the day, blinking at the harsh rays of the dying sun as it fought ferociously to stay blazing, creating such a spectacle of dancing fire in the colorful heavens that everyone was aware of, but very few appreciated until the night came.

___

I remember watching a modern video essay on the impermanence of life and importance of memory. Made by a young audience, the young man and his peers had only just graduated high school, telling tales of hope and cliches wrapped up in admittedly excellent cinematography, hooks and editing. In it, with typical Gen-Z flair, there was a scene in which the protagonist of the story lies with a girl his age in the same bed, both looking up at us (the camera is recording at a birds-eye view). We are given little context to how they got there, but the obvious implication they want us to assume is made even more plain:

"Hey, do you think you'll remember me in five years?" asks the boy.

The girl considered briefly for a moment, her eyes closed. "Yeah, I think so, but you'll probably be just that guy I was fooling around with. Like, I might remember you as Justin and think, 'Oh, right. Justin. That guy was nice. He had nice freckles and a cool laugh.'"

The boy seems to accept this response as an invitation to continue, which he does eagerly with "Then if you'll remember me in five years, do you think you'll remember me in twenty years?" He shifts to look at the girl, whose eyes are still shut.

"Maybe I will, yeah. But it'll probably be even more vague than that."

The pair lay for some time in silence, which the boy promptly breaks with his next question.

"Do you think it's better to remember or to forget?"

The girl does not respond. The boy shifts to look at her once more and believes her to be asleep.

"Yeah, me too."

___

Some years passed, and my family moved to Massachusetts. My voice deepened and I slowly encountered more and more about the world. Some experiences would be particularly enlightening to me, and one of them happened on a windy day heading to my father's workplace with a friend. We crossed the street to promptly turn a corner and run into a group among them, chief among them a man of note. The man was rather shabbily dressed, with patchworked fragments of clothing, sitting on a metal chair outside a closed building. Around him sat some of his compatriots, sprawled across ornately designed chairs and rough glass tables, some with cigarettes in their mouths or feet kicked up. Now, having grown a little in both the body and mind, I still felt a twinge of guilt that seems mandatory when I passed by less fortunate people. With all that, I would've simply walked away from them without a second look like so many before, except I saw with them this shabbily dressed man.

And froze for he had a prosthetic foot.

There was perhaps a stunned second where I believed that it could be the same man from the station that night. But he bore very little resemblance to that man; whereas the man that night was black and scarred, this one was milky white and scraggly. There was still defiance in this one's eyes, unlike the eyes of the other, where it seemed as though the glazed quality of his eyes came from not only a horror he had endured, but witnessed with his own eyes without being able to squeeze his own eyes shut. Not to mention the wrong leg. After my brain had reconciled these facts, I was about to foolishly open my mouth to speak to the man, but to no avail as my friend dragged me back along. 

To the group of men, it must've seemed like we wanted little to do with them and went on our way, and their memory of us likely faded within days.

___

Despite all the hallmarks of a short film cooked up for a cinematography class, many viewers found the young man's project rather gripping; despite all my slander for its cringiness, I must agree. This little skit, however, was particularly insightful for me. In spite of all its cliches, I must praise the project directors for instilling this question deep into my mind, and the boy's very content answer. All the same, I think of moments I think I would rather forget sometimes, fights with people that I cherished but are now disconnected from me, times where I did not do someone credit, when I have slandered people behind their backs, and I feel guilty, even while knowing that my mortality's mistakes are inevitable.

All of this thinking leads me back to the question. If concepts like samsara are real, the loss of all my memories from my previous lives is something I greatly despise. Isn't it better, I plead in a wondrous voice like a child who has hopes of sanctity and heroism from his beloved parents, a sanctiminious voice that proclaims my class as one who has never really experienced that sort of pain, to have experienced everything and come out stronger?

___

"'Tis better to have loved and lost than never loved at all," I said.

"For this scenario, I disagree," she said.

What two people is this story about again?
