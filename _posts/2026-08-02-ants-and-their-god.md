## "If an ant carries an object a hundred times its weight, you can carry burdens many times your size." - Matshona Dhliwayo

I broke up with my girlfriend roughly four months ago. It was a mutually agreed upon decision with few fireworks like the teary breakups of old; in reality, we had of course drifted apart months prior and were only called a couple by name or from others. There were many reasons for this, but primary among them was our propensity to never understand each other because our realities differed so widely. We will come back to this in a little while, after a deeper exploration of what I mean when I say ‘realities’.

Today it feels like individuals simply choose the reality they wish to exist within. Information, news, and facts are less of a meal and more of a buffet; we can take what we want back to our tables, ignore the rest, and proceed through our lives. It seems the only thing we could all agree on is that so-called echo chambers are bad. This alone is strange, because we can't agree on who is actually in an echo chamber. To Group A, Group B is the guilty party; to Group B, Group A is instead. The easiest diagnosis here is that people simply do not hear opinions from an opposing side, but that is in fact a completely different phenomenon than what is occurring today. As well-trodden as this subject may seem, we have a fundamental misunderstanding of echo chambers. Media literacy will be an important piece of this puzzle, as will a third point perhaps more extraordinary, where the true aggressors go seemingly unaccused.

C. Thi Nguyen is a professor at the University of Utah whose research largely focuses on how social structures manipulate rationality and information processing. In 2020, he published a paper called "Echo Chambers and Epistemic Bubbles." By grasping the distinction between these two phenomena, we can get closer to a full understanding of true echo chambers. By Nguyen's estimation, many people use the term "echo chamber" when they are in fact discussing epistemic bubbles. Although it may feel like an exercise in semantics, the distinction between epistemic bubbles and echo chambers matters a lot. By understanding our structures and systems of knowledge, we are better equipped to uncover their shortcomings and identify if we are in fact in an echo chamber. Of course, the more we understand something, the easier it then becomes to dismantle.

Our world is set up in a way which requires us to lean on outside sources for knowledge. There is too much information in the world, and despite what internet rabbit holes may lead us to believe, a given individual is not a subject-matter expert in almost anything, hence we must rely on third parties. We know it is bad to smoke cigarettes not because we are experts on the human respiratory system; instead, those people who are have done the research and can tell us this is true. We know to avoid drinking bleach not because we are experts on toxicology, but instead because the people who are have informed us not to drink bleach. We don't know exercise is good because we're all sports scientists. We don't know our brakes stop our cars because we're all expert mechanics, etc.

However, these types of structures aren't always bad. As Nguyen points out, if you were an anti-Nazi in Nazi Germany, you would be correct to exist within an anti-Nazi echo chamber; you would do well to reject the prevailing consensus based solely on the fact that it was so prevalent. The problem occurs when we begin to manipulate and exploit these concepts.

As Nguyen says, an epistemic bubble is a social structure which excludes relevant information and voices through omission. That is to say, these bubbles lack aggression or even ill intent. We stay in touch with our friends who have similar worldviews to ours; we draw from similar news sources as people we like or respect. Speaking on philosophical matters to my friends, many share a similar outlook of a mix of hedonism, realism, and a ‘we’ll figure it out’ attitude that much of my age group shares. Academically, we share news about our teachers, circulating good or bad news about them. We share ideologies in social structure, in self-help. This gives us a filtered sort of worldview which omits contrary views and artificially inflates our own self-confidence. Epistemic bubbles are bad, but they are not malicious and are more likely just a negative side effect of the way we are required to interface with the world. Thankfully, epistemic bubbles are easy to break down because people do not actively crusade against other opinions, so as we introduce new opinions we find a population that is open to them. By injecting new ideas into epistemic bubbles, we have basically solved the problem.

However, I would argue that epistemic bubbles are pretty rare, especially in online discourse. By far the more common are echo chambers. In an echo chamber, relevant opposing voices are actively discredited. These structures work by intentionally isolating members from outside sources, resulting in an inflated dependence on insider authority figures. In rejecting all outside sources of information, we get what can best be described as cult-like behavior. There is one leader, or a cadre of leaders, who all have the ultimate knowledge and keys to a so-described truth or reality.

Echo chambers present a number of self-strengthening traits which make them almost impossible to untangle. The first is inside language: echo chambers employ their own sort of slang. People who know or understand a certain set of words are insiders, and those who reject this language are outsiders. This is a simple tactic, but this mounting distance from outsiders then produces another hallmark of the echo chamber: disagreement reinforcement.

This mechanism is the armor that echo chambers so effectively wear. The leader of an echo chamber tells its members that the rest of the world hates the group for knowing some truth, and that outsiders will label members as crazy, cultish, or lunatics. The leader says that outsiders will tell the echo chamber's members that they are incorrect. This is a clever trick, because when one takes false beliefs into the world, the world will absolutely confront an individual on those grounds; however, with the disagreement-reinforcement mechanism, those confrontations serve only to strengthen a member's faith in their leaders. "He told me you would say that, so he must be smart, credible, correct," etc. This is why factual information does basically nothing to shatter echo chambers: those within the chamber are already prepared to hear and reject information. Of course, I am not saying that some echo chambers aren't centered around specific people but it is hardly necessary for an echo chamber to form. More to the point, individuals are prepared in a way that opposition just galvanizes their belief in the echo chamber.

More recently, a longstanding group who have been communicating digitally split with one of its members who had gone rogue and seized its history for themselves. Their motive is unknown to me and yet they refuse to chalk it up to a bad temper and instead choose to double down on their decision. To some of the others in the group, the behavior was not out of the question. The member had acted out radically and had made many indications of similar behavior in the past. To others, such a move was completely nonsensical and lacked rationale. Indeed, from the outside, it seems much like a temper tantrum, what with their refusal to share their motive and their petty grievances… or so it would seem to the rest of us. To this person, their reason for doing such things that were labeled as insane by others seemed perfectly normal and rational, perhaps even in retrospect. Here then, is an interesting case: the ‘factual information’ that we speak of in this case will most certainly be rejected by the rogue member, because the rogue member has ‘false beliefs’ themselves. Regardless of whether or not the motive is valid, the premise holds; both parties are self-assured in their own correctness, although this delves into ethics as well. Nevertheless, it is still clear how we have the self-sustaining nature of these structures: each time outsiders try to deploy factual information, they just strengthen the barrier between the echo chamber and outside reality. Attempts to break them down only fortify their walls.

Echo chambers exist as perversions of a reasonable knowledge system, disregarding the information and instead placing emphasis on the authority figure or the falsehoods they spout. Conversely, it is not the opposing facts which members reject, but often the people who are presenting them. When we discuss the false notion that the 2020 election was stolen, those who believe this often reject not the material facts at hand, but CNN, NBC, Reuters, or whoever is speaking about them. In a healthy epistemic community, as Nguyen says, there is an upper ceiling on the credibility of any individual. If our plumber tells us we need a new toilet, we believe him. If our plumber tells us that aliens are coming from Mars and sabotaging our toilet while we sleep, not so much. If we applied an echo chamber to the aforementioned situation, we would be perfectly happy to accept this bizarre, alien-based diagnosis. This phenomenon is called runaway credence: the structure of echo chambers allows not fact, but instead the individual delivering information, to reign supreme, because trust is not in information. Information alone cannot break down an echo chamber, especially when we consider the disagreement-reinforcement mechanism.

The solutions to echo chambers are surely complex, disparate, and would take years to implement. The contributing factors to the phenomenon are equally so - social media algorithms, a justified mistrust in our government, human psychology, to name a few - and I can't claim to be an expert on any of these things by my own point. However, having spoken with a few curious adults who are experts on a different topic, namely media literacy, I feel fairly confident in my ability to regurgitate them.

What is media literacy? Educator Elizabeth Thoman put it well when she said media literacy is the ability to create personal meaning from the verbal and visual symbols we take in every day. It's the ability to choose and select, to challenge and question, to be conscious about what is going on around us and not be passive and vulnerable.

This is not a new conversation. In 1933, Leavis and Thompson proposed teaching UK students how to distinguish between forms of popular culture. In the 1970s, the American government and private sectors mirrored this protectionism and financially supported media literacy education in schools through critical viewing skills curricula. When we look into the programs and the people who led them, we see an underfunded and haphazard implementation. Indeed, these media literacy programs never became more than a passing fad. The 1990s also saw a piecemeal resurgence in media literacy education, but again, to no real significant end.

Today, scholars point largely to the movement's protectionist approach for its failure. American media literacy education painted the advent of television broadly as a danger to young people, without highlighting the medium's tremendous educational upsides as well. All of these half-hearted attempts are partly to blame for our current predicament, but I would also suggest that our collective ideas about media literacy have remained the same while media itself has changed drastically.
In the past, media had the power to change culture, but it existed outside of culture. There existed times when people with differing media diets got along quite magnanimously. However, now media is culture. If you consume Fox News, or CNN, or the BBC, that alone is a cultural signifier. Unfortunately, things like Twitter follows say something very real and meaningful about you as a person. The internet fundamentally changed our media landscape, while our ideas about media literacy have really remained stagnant. Troublesome though it is to admit, and we will discuss this later, the modern citizen must be new-media literate to participate responsibly in our society. But before we can understand a framework for new media literacy, we must understand the new media landscape.

Traditional mediums still exist, of course, and they still have some influence, but it is reasonable to declare that modern media is largely on the internet - Web 2.0, if I may. Before our current era of internet technology, we existed with something like a Web 1.0. This early type of internet technology is often described as "read only" because there was very little participation from the user. Instead, in Web 1.0, the user could consume digital formats, communicate one-to-one through email and chat rooms, and do basic creative work; however, these users could not really participate en masse in internet content creation. In the early 2000s, social networking sites began to appear; comment sections, likes, favorites, and reposts by users became cornerstones of the online experience. We are now in Web 2.0, and the read-only internet is dead. It is now easy for a user to make their voice heard, to adopt identities through online communities, and to participate in the construction of ideas themselves. This is in many ways a good thing. For one, our online diet is not monopolized by large corporations, nor do we have the problem of bad-faith gatekeepers. Although the idea of meritocracy is perhaps fantastical, Web 2.0 has made creative work into something much closer to this utopian ideal. Individuals can contribute works to communities which are receptive to them; this has developed strong incentives for creative expression. We can publish things to people who very well may like them, thus encouraging more people to create more things freely. Great ideas are no longer shackled by technical knowledge. We have seen movement within the arts and culture like never before, a mass cultural movement that has, in general, been quite positive.

However, Web 2.0 has had immense negative impacts on our information culture. Within Web 1.0, users were generally consumers of media produced by experts because it was the traditional publishing environment. But in 2.0, the non-expert consumers are also the producers; users can collectively publish and echo any bit of information which they find acceptable, regardless of how subject-matter experts may feel about the material. The unfortunate result is that Web 2.0 is so rife with misinformation published by unqualified individuals that attempting to weed through the true and untrue is a seemingly impossible task. Furthermore, as Web 2.0 users can develop social communities around this information, media has become more than a mover of culture but rather culture itself.

Nguyen has also published [a piece](https://philarchive.org/archive/NGUMOP) on a new phenomenon which has emerged with Web 2.0: the idea of pornography. Nguyen describes pornography as when an image is sexual pornography when we use it for immediate gratification while avoiding the complexities of actual sexual relationships such as physical intimacy and emotional connection. As our identities become wrapped up in online media, so has emerged a new version of pornography. One form this new version takes on is "food porn", images of delicious food we engage with without worrying about the labor of cooking, the effects of calorie or nutritional intake, the availability of ingredients themselves, or any other factors. But by far the most relevant to our discussion, we now have "outrage porn”, information we engage with for emotional stimulation while avoiding the practical realities of that information itself. This news story makes you feel angry about it, and it is that feeling which we pursue, rather than the factuality, bias, or subtext of the information presented. Nguyen points to the traditional problem with sexual pornography in that it portrays sex in wildly unrealistic terms, and consumers are at risk of exporting those terms into real sex. So too is the risk with outrage pornography, coloring how we perceive the world around us in a way that is emblematic of failed media literacy. It bears noting how far our society is down the well that I am drawing this analogy from the media apparatus as a whole to illicit adult content. Sickeningly, the analogy makes complete sense.

So the question is then: how do we update our version of media literacy to meet a new modern standard?

___

In 2013, Tzu-Bin Lin, Jen-Yi Li, Feng Deng, and Ling Lee published [a paper called "Understanding New Media Literacy: An Explorative Theoretical Framework”](https://www.jstor.org/stable/jeductechsoci.16.4.160), presenting a plan for new media literacy consisting of five parts: consuming, understanding, analysis, synthesis, and evaluation. Each of these steps is remarkably simple, but the sum of these parts creates a solid strategy for media consumption.

The **consuming** skill is the simple technical ability to absorb media such as how to operate a computer, how to locate information, and how to broadly use the internet as a companion. Next, the researchers point to the fundamental ability to understand media: we should be able to **understand** the new-era language used by new-era media; we should be able to read, parse, and understand sentences as they are presented; we must understand what a headline or a passage means, regardless of the platform on which it is published. Modern media has its own slang and shorthand that we should know.

Most people already have a grasp of consuming and understanding, so it is in the following steps where there is a need for growth. In the third step, **analysis**, the individual must be able to perform surface-level criticism of media messages. We must understand when language is coded, when bias is presented, and when certain sides of an issue are omitted. We should understand who is presenting a piece of media and what their relationship to the issue at hand may be. At its core, we must perceive media not as neutral conveyors of objective reality, recognizing that all media is the result of subjective, socially driven processes. More specifically, the arrival of media at our attention is certainly subjective: why are we seeing this news story, and which circuits are we plugged into that push a given piece of media to our attention?

From here we move into the more treacherous realm of **synthesis**. This refers to the user's ability to internally remix media and oppose it against, or integrate it responsibly with, one's own viewpoints. If CNN tells us something, how does it reconcile with our core beliefs and knowledge? Do we inherently agree or disagree, and why? This is not necessarily an immediate quest for some objective truth but rather the acknowledgement that a given piece of media carries with it subtexts and implications. We don't necessarily need to reject media on this bias, but instead appreciate that it exists and bring these subtexts into how we parse media.

The final step on the path is **evaluation**. When we have equipped ourselves with the aforementioned pieces, what do we get? This is a difficult step, and one that many people, myself included, must consistently remind ourselves of. We must pose a piece of media against concepts like motivation, identities, power dynamics, and ideologies. This step involves a decision-making process which the others do not: we must ultimately decide to what extent we will accept or reject the information itself, not solely the parties publishing it. Media outfits have embedded ideologies and power dynamics which greatly influence what these outfits produce, but the media itself also has embedded purposes beyond relaying information. We must recognize all of these and consider to what extent we will accept or reject any given piece of material.

I know these steps and processes all sound incredibly simple in theory, maybe even intuitive, but they have not been applied to the new media landscape, and so it is appropriate to begin with the basics. But to me, there is a bigger question: why do we need to do any of this?
The above frameworks are certainly helpful in pursuing a modern version of media literacy, but the frustrating truth is that they are in many ways unrealistic. A microscopic portion of internet users will see this, and even fewer (perhaps none) will ever read the papers discussed herein. It would be beyond naive to deploy this information and believe that everyone who uses the internet will fundamentally change their habits. It would be hoping for a small solution to a big problem.

That problem, it seems to me, is this: those in power in America refuse to take responsibility for their actions, for the nature of the world they've built. Whether we discuss media literacy, echo chambers, division, or the swift rise of extreme ideology in the United States, our leaders seem dumbfounded, looking around, grasping stupidly at rhetoric and surface-level solutions, wondering how things got this way. But these leaders need only look in the mirror. The U.S. government has routinely lied to its population; they have deceived us about foreign and domestic policy; they act on behalf of special-interest groups, their own power positions, their bank accounts, and global American supremacy. As they proceed in this way, these individuals construct illusions and tales about the ostensible goodness of their deeds.

Moreover, the government has allowed tech companies to act with near autonomy. Today, these technocrats play with our economy and our culture like puppeteers, permitted to manipulate the public, create destructive algorithms, and foment hatred, all in the pursuit of never-ending capital growth. More traditional media outlets are too empowered to act with wild indiscretion; indeed, the largest news channels in our country spew incendiary stories to the public which are laden with omissions and misleading information. These media companies are driven to steal your attention and pour their audiences out to multinational corporations, corporations which often own those news outlets themselves. It should be no wonder that our relationship to media is in such decay. After all, a structure so extraordinary can only produce extraordinary results.

Obviously, people have free agency and they can do things and not do things, they can choose their own information diet. Surely, for any given individual, it may be possible to successfully navigate modern media, but we should not be so presumptuous as to assume that broadly we can expect this from the population in a widespread way. People have jobs and responsibilities, they are talented in some ways and obtuse in others. We cannot expect everyone to spend their days combing through research on media literacy; this is an absurd notion, considered alongside the media apparatuses that we are posed against. Tech companies plunge billions of dollars and the world's most intelligent minds into developing their algorithms; they have armies dedicated to the continued operation of their endeavors, behemoths which plow forward with only capital in mind. We are not David fighting Goliath. No, this would be far too generous. We are instead like an ant fighting God Himself.

If those in power wish to keep faith in the invisible hand to create our media landscape, so be it, but those same people must empower the citizens to function within that landscape. Like so many of our problems, media literacy and echo chambers are the result of structures put in place enacted by those in power. Top-down problems require top-down solutions. We must earnestly dedicate resources to media literacy programs in schools and educate the youth on how to function in our hailstorm of information. I believe we may need to seriously assess how we distribute time and resources in our public education systems. Should a teenager be spending their time on advanced trigonometry while ignoring the most basic skills required to function in our world? More to the point, should these skills be relegated to extracurriculars and optional electives? Indeed, new media literacy is a required skill, for our own systems have created a world which is largely digital, and so those same systems must empower individuals to function successfully within it, lest we languish and wallow in the obvious failures of what exists now. As things stand, our media landscape is a game authored by individuals who have not distributed instructions on how to actually play, and still, after many rounds, the creators look around like foolish people, wondering why no one is able to win this game they have built, wondering why the board is in such disarray. It is truly an absurdity.

___

Some months ago, I was tuna fishing off the coast of California. We fished for nearly a day on the dark blue water, and of the dozen people on our boat, no one caught anything. This long period of inactivity meant we spent most of our time talking to one another. I spoke to one man who insisted that the COVID-19 pandemic had been somehow planned by the government, and that the vaccines were full of tracking devices. At that moment, the level of ignorance displayed by the man hit me with wild frustration. I did not press the issue, for I had that debilitating form of shock which comes from being met with information that was so removed from reality that no argument could ever make any progress. It was like talking to a stereotypical flat earther about the concept of geocentrism.

Later, I spoke to my friend Selene on the drive home from the harbor. We had no fish to speak of, so with a heated sort of anger, I told her about the conversation with the man, telling her that the man wasn't even functioning within reality. Selene returned that, to him, the fisherman was indeed living in reality. What he described was his reality, that no matter how bizarre it may have seemed, the information he espoused was as real to him as the sky being blue or the grass being green.

So too it was with my ex-girlfriend. Due to the different forms of media we consumed, the different ways we were brought up, the different environments at home and around home, we developed very different viewpoints on the world on many, if not most topics. As such, we existed in different echo chambers, having come from different walks of life. Unlike epistemic bubbles, I felt I could not introduce new ideas that weren’t already ‘pre-approved’ topics through trial and error, and I am fairly sure she thought the same. There exists a gulf of understanding between me and the fisherman, between me and my ex, and it is perhaps a gap I will never be able to close. But it is the same everywhere, between the Ku Klux Klan and the Black population in America, between the African beauty standard of mouth flaps and the Western Aryan one, between hedonistic and nihilistic lifestyles. In my eyes, all we can do is try and make peace with that.

Selene was right. Individuals today have the ability to select and construct a reality which suits them. They may live in this alternate dimension, which is just as real to them as anything may be to me. This is not to justify the man's position or his ignorance but rather to reflect on the nature of our world, which now consists of endless different realities. This is a problem. I obviously cannot say that I possess a panacea, and this essay really only scratches the surface of this whole issue, but in my eyes, if we address how to interface with the world, we can address the world itself.
