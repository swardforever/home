## “God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him. Yet his shadow still looms. How shall we comfort ourselves, the murderers of all murderers?” -Nietzsche, Thus Spoke Zarathustra

##### This post contains thinking on pain and death. If you are sensitive or uncomfortable with the topics stated, please do not read this post.

There is a parable, older than most literature we still read, called Appointment in Samarra. I first encountered it retold by Maugham (the first story [here](https://www.emmanuelacc.net/sundayroundtable3.pdf), though I believe its roots reach back into old Mesopotamian and Arabic folklore.

The story goes like this. A merchant in Baghdad sends his servant to the marketplace to buy provisions. The servant returns pale and trembling; he tells his master that in the crowd, there was a grim figure dressed in long robes, the reaper Death jostling him. Death made a threatening gesture toward him. Terrified, the servant begs to borrow his master's fastest horse so that he might flee to Samarra, a city far away, where Death will surely not find him. The master agrees, and the servant rides off as fast as the horse will carry him. The merchant however, becomes curious, and himself goes down to the marketplace and finds Death still standing there, milling about in the crowd. After working up his nerve, he asks why Death frightened his servant with such a menacing gesture. Death replies that it was merely a start of surprise, because he was astonished to see the servant in Baghdad that morning, when he had an appointment with him that very night, in Samarra.

___

Often I think about dying. To toot my own horn a little bit, I consider myself a fairly intelligent and reflective person. I'm capable of forming coherent thoughts on a number of subjects, but thoughts of death shatter me entirely. I once tried, at the ripe age of 13, to think about death for twenty minutes straight. I was horrified. Having just tried the experiment again, I am in no way more equipped than I was when I was 13. In the words of Leo Tolstoy, I realize that "as I live, I am being stuffed into a big dark sack."

Why do we die? What does death mean? Why do we live if we must die? If we must die, why do we live? These questions are perhaps the most well-trodden of all; history's greatest thinkers have pondered over death. There is no shortage of thought and media on it. And yet we still have no answers.

As far as I can tell, there are two places we can seek an answer to death, those being man and nature. Man presents us with science, and on the subject of death, science offers a white flag in a war, telling us the biomechanical and physiological realities of death, what happens to your body when you die, how your body decays. While academically interesting, this does little to bring clarity to death nor help someone find peace with it. By that token, the world's greatest scientists are rendered somehow into uselessness.

Moving past science, we can look elsewhere, namely spiritualists and philosophers. The might of all of this intellectual force has been hurled at death time and time again. What I find in these works is simply lots of questions, but no answers. Surely I can expect nothing more, people are writing about things they know nothing about. They so beautifully theorized about death, but they had not died, and by proxy they were as ignorant then as I am now. These writings are window dressing, beautiful, but ultimately without function in the face of death, and being shoved into a sack, do I want it to have spots or stripes?

Of course, there's also God, religion, man's greatest shield against the reality of death. I have a deep admiration for the religious, to have such faith, such conviction in a storm of arguments to the contrary. I believe this requires a tremendous and unique strength of character, but like so many, I cannot bring myself to believe in God. I'd like to believe in God, but I'm not strong enough. I cannot look at the world around us, even with all of its beauty, and take seriously the idea of God. What God gives a child cancer? What God sends hurricanes to the poor? Oh, God of mine, religion can have no place in my conversation with death.

Without other solutions, I often look to nature. Here we have vultures, hyenas, insects; these creatures all thrive off the deaths of others. In nature, animals die and leave opportunities for the other members of their ecosystem. I do find some visceral beauty in this phenomenon, but this does not provide me with some great comfort, and I still find myself running into that same concrete wall. A conclusion of nature does nothing to abate the paralyzing feeling I get when I think about death. Reason and hope become grotesquely meaningless when posed with these questions. The truth, the truth that I know with all of my body, is that there can be no answer. In fact, the only answer is the hopelessness I feel when I think about death. How can a person really live with death looming over everything, at the end of every day? Many philosophers and authors have discussed this question. Unlike their conversations of death, their conversations of life in the face of death can provide some earnest direction.

In 1879, Leo Tolstoy had already published War and Peace as well as Anna Karenina. Such works earned him a place among the world's literary greats, and he was hailed as a genius. After being born into wealth, Tolstoy had become famous for doing what he loved the most, yet he felt entirely unfulfilled. He was spiritually and emotionally lost, despite a life that should have produced the opposite.

It was in this state that he wrote [A Confession](https://ia800402.us.archive.org/17/items/Confession-LevTolstoy/Confession%20-%20Lev%20Tolstoy.pdf). This roughly hundred-page essay outlined his quest to find the meaning of life, and specifically the deep depression that he fell into upon concluding that life was meaningless. It discusses melancholy, suicide, depression, misery; it is certainly one of the most heartbreaking, arresting essays ever put to paper. The confession begins with his decreasing interest and belief in religion. He says that he only believed in God because his elders had instructed him to, and even then the belief had been shaky. Throughout Tolstoy's early years, perhaps his truer religion had been art. He sought fulfillment in his art and in the circles of geniuses that he associated with, he had once held a great faith in the arts, but it was one that began to wane.

The people in his circle, including himself, were paid and praised highly for their work. The public said they were arbiters of profound, ultimate truths, that these artists were enlightening the world. The artists themselves believed it: how could they not? But Tolstoy eventually realized that for every great writer's idea, there would be another who wholly disagreed with it. So he wondered how any of these people could really be arbiters of truth if they themselves didn't even agree on that truth. Tolstoy observed that his peers simply spent time with people who praised them and rejected those who didn't, so every artist believed themselves to be correct. His faith in the arts as a driver of meaning crumbled. Instead, he began to look at the concept of progress, that one could find fulfillment by living to make progress within progress. Tolstoy believed he would have a family, create a wondrous life, and slowly create some form of progress within himself.

But years into this process, Tolstoy traveled to Paris, where he witnessed a public execution: a prisoner's head fell into its box. Tolstoy began to question this concept of progress. This disillusionment continued with the death of Tolstoy's brother, Nikolai. He was a kind and intelligent man; when he fell ill in his mid-twenties, Nikolai suffered for over a year, eventually succumbing to an agonizing death from consumption (otherwise known as a severe tuberculosis). But in Tolstoy's estimation, Nikolai never understood why he lived, why he suffered, nor why he died. In the face of this reality, what good was progress? Still clinging to this idea of progress, Tolstoy began to teach young peasants in Russia. However, he quickly realized this was futile: Tolstoy himself did not have what they needed to learn. Whatever knowledge would have improved their lives and provided them fulfillment, Tolstoy himself could not identify, and didn't even have. Therefore his teaching too became an impossible task.

These experiences all crystallized into a deep, dark time of depression for Leo Tolstoy. In A Confession, he says on his pursuit of progress that he still had not understood that in answering that one must live according to progress, he was talking just like a person being carried along in a boat by the waves and the wind. Such a person replies to the only important question, "Where do we steer?" by saying, "We are being carried somewhere."

Tolstoy had always thought the meaning of life and death would come to him, and he would find it eventually. But now it became clear this wasn't going to happen. These questions came up over and over in his mind. In the confession, he compares them to tiny black points, for they were few and far between initially, but were coming together to form a single, unignorable dark stain. They were, he said, like an illness ignored by a patient. The symptoms occurred with increasing frequency until they turned into one continuous duration of suffering. Suddenly the patient would discover the truth, that what was once a mild illness had now become death.

Tolstoy was a famous intellectual. He had access to the greatest knowledge history had ever produced because no library would refuse him entry. He could sit down and have conversations with the wisest men in the world, and so he did. Through all of this, he found no answers and was instead overtaken with an intense depression. His thoughts turned to suicide, and suicidal ideation became as normal to him as self-preservation once had been. It is apparent that the temptation to end his life felt as natural as eating or sleeping.

To prevent himself from taking his own life, Tolstoy removed all the ropes from his room and stopped hunting with a gun. He likened this time to having climbed a mountain: he was as successful as he could have ever been, he had reached the summit, expecting a wondrous view. Instead he stood there like a fool, having found only an empty wasteland.

In time, Tolstoy found that he didn't have the answers to life and death, but instead that these questions simply didn't have answers. Tolstoy concludes that there are four ways to live a life, at least for a man in his class.

The first was ignorance. Some people, for whatever reason, had never been bothered by these questions. Tolstoy's thoughts here were simple: once you start thinking about these things, you can't unthink them, hence there wasn't anything he could learn from the ignorant. The second was epicureanism, being aware of one's impending death and its consequences, but enjoying life anyway (commonly adapted as hedonism). He points to the words of the biblical figure King Solomon, to do whatever you can do by the strength of your hand, for there is no work in the grave where you are going. Tolstoy writes that most people employ this method, but he points to it as being inherently immoral. He says that these people lead lives where they experience more good things than bad, but through a kind of moral stupidity, they forget that all of their advantages and privileges are pure luck; they just happen to be born into a good situation. He says they forget that not everyone can be a King Solomon with his palaces, his women, and his money; for every palace there are a thousand men who built it. The same chance that turns a man into a Solomon one day can turn him into one of Solomon's slaves the next. Tolstoy says that these people have a dullness of imagination that allows them to forget death, to forget that death will eventually destroy the things they enjoy so much. He claims epicureans aren't really much different than the ignorant: once a person is illuminated to the moral problems with this lifestyle, there's again no going back. Ultimately, Tolstoy finds no comfort in epicureanism.

The third choice he proposes is suicide. If you don't like a room, then leave, Tolstoy declares. However, he says he is too emotionally weak to do this.

The final option is where I find comfort, and it seems Tolstoy found some version of comfort as well. That which it is to simply live, to recognize that everything will be destroyed eventually, and that you will die eventually, but to keep moving forward, no matter how painfully inane or seemingly pointless death renders things to be. One may wonder how this differs from the second perspective. 

Tolstoy wrote this essay in the midst of a profound depression, and so he concludes that option four is wholly miserable. However, I have to disagree. I don't choose to disagree; I have to. I have no interest in suicide. I have no way of understanding death - after all, to the living, this question will be forever unanswered. So all that I can do is find fulfillment in life, in a meaningful connection to the act of living. This house may be destroyed in time, but I must still live in it, so I should make it nice. And so, when I torment myself with imaginations of death, I have to seek fulfillment in the mundane and the everyday. I cannot dwell on the future. Friends, family, work, leisure, these are the things that make up my life, however futile they are when pitted against death. Indeed, the alternative is horrific: to let death lead one's life entirely astray, to ignore the issues of death and lead a wholly unexamined life. This is an entirely different form of self-destruction.

Perhaps appropriately, this is a subject Tolstoy examined in another work.

___

[The Death of Ivan Ilyich](https://open.lib.umn.edu/ivanilich/chapter/full-text-english/) is a fantastic companion piece to the confession, whether or not it was intended as such. It tells the story of Ivan Ilyich, a court official who falls and injures himself while hanging curtains in his home. The pain grows until a physician informs Ivan that the injury is terminal and he will die. The novella tells the story of Ivan's death; in fact, it opens with his funeral. From the first pages, the reader is assured that nothing will save Ivan Ilyich. The book does not end with some miracle.

Ivan Ilyich spent his life as basically a social climber and a careerist. On his deathbed, he learns the consequences of this life. As Ivan lies dying, he remembers a passage he studied in school: "Caius is a man, all men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal." Slowly, Ivan realizes that he has ignored death, and instead has lived a profound lie, an existence with no examination of death. Like a school child who breaks down when faced with punishment, Ivan's lie also crumbles as death becomes imminent. His concealed death from himself thereby concealed his life as well.

Ivan, in the early stages of his affliction, wants only to live how he used to, to go back to when things were pleasant, before this whole mess. So he begins to reflect on these pleasantries, but as he lies in his bed dying, Ivan realizes they all feel quite different. He says these pleasantries are melting into insignificance except for the earliest recollections of his childhood. Of course, it was during these years that Ivan was unaffected by formal ideas of pleasantness or societal expectations.

It occurs to Ivan that perhaps he did not live his life correctly. He asks, "How can that be? I did everything that was demanded of me." And in this question, Ivan is presented with the truth: he lived his entire life to fulfill the whims of others, in some cases, in the micro sense, his employers or co-workers, and in others, more grand, the expectations and ambitions that culture imposed on him, things he fell for e.g. wealth, status, adornment. As early as his schooling days, Ivan says he committed acts that he found abhorrent, that inspired in him a sense of self-hatred, but by his colleagues these acts were considered normal and admirable. Eventually, Ivan himself became one of those colleagues - these abhorrent acts likewise became normal, and even aspirational, by virtue of other people's expectations. So his life met all of these expectations, earning him the approval and admiration of these people and institutions. But Tolstoy writes that Ivan's chief agony, one that even the opium could not solve, was the sickening feeling that his whole life, Ivan had not been doing the right thing.

Eventually, Ivan cedes to this agony and its inevitable answer, but by then all of his decisions had already inflicted their damage. On his deathbed, Ivan is truly alone, profoundly alone, in a way unbeholden to the physical presence of other humans. Tolstoy writes that Ivan feels a loneliness among his acquaintances and family, people who he had believed to be his loved ones but perhaps are not. Of Ivan's loneliness, Tolstoy says it is fuller than any that can exist on earth, at the bottom of the sea, or in the ground itself.

Ivan's death, because of the way he lived, becomes an experience of isolation, and so at his deathbed he is surrounded only by the most shallow version of company. In one passage, Ivan wants nothing more than to cry, to weep, and for someone to pity him as he lies in his excruciatingly painful death. He wants someone to cry with him, to comfort him as if he were a child. This is all that he wants with every fiber of his being, and then in would come one of his colleagues, asking only for a ruling on a legal matter. All around him, Ivan saw people who were lying; they were basically pretending he wasn't actually dying. Tolstoy writes that this lie, all around him and inside him, more than anything poisoned the last days of Ivan Ilyich's life.

When death finally arrives for Ivan, he begs his family for forgiveness. He cries and pleads, and only in this does he then die in peace. But Ivan's funeral reveals the depravity of his life in one final and horrifically triumphant blow. The attendees do not mourn or cry, but rather express their own selfishness. Some think of the promotions they may win now that he's died, and others consider what amounts of money they could inherit. Still more people seem entirely annoyed by the whole thing, annoyed by the tedium of the tasks they must now perform. Broadly, these attendees are filled with a subdued joy, joy that it is Ivan who has died, not them.
The Death of Ivan Ilyich tells us that a life poorly lived only serves to amplify the horrors of death, or perhaps create them entirely. And so I believe the answer to death is not found in what happens after, or during, or even why we all must die, but instead in how we live.

___

The Übermensch is a philosophical ideal proposed by Nietzsche in his famous work [Thus Spoke Zarathustra](https://www.nothuman.net/images/files/discussion/1/9994c4af16851fe2bc691b8896cf6694.pdf). It details the ideal human that Nietzsche envisions, someone who rejects religious frameworks and embraces life in every aspect. He writes much about the Übermensch and how to achieve this state but one noteworthy aspect about it is that the Übermensch possesses a quality known as ‘amor fati’, or the love of one’s fate, in all its pieces. It mirrors stoicism of Aurelian views very well. While you cannot control the events that happen around you, you can control your own reaction to it. By this extension, there is no worth in thinking about the past in a negative light and spewing logorrhea about what bitter choices and events happened in the past, or for the matter, the present. It is the concept of amor fati that I find myself attached to, that which I think is the best way to live.

Death can be a lot of things, an enemy, a pair of shackles, a companion. My dread will never go away. I will never have answers to the question of death. I shall still lie in bed and think about dying with regularity, likely for the rest of my life. I dare not delude myself with ideas of legacy, for all people die, some just take longer.

Tales like that of the merchant’s servant, running to Samarra to try to escape his fate and meeting it all the same, do not persuade me that running from death is a good idea. He resigned himself to his death the moment he felt his life was in danger. Rather, it is imperative to me that I should live a beautiful, vivid life, without cowering from pain, or loss, or difficulty, or even death, else there is no point in said death at all, merely a transition from one monotony to the next. This principle perhaps illustrates two points I have on death: first, that I can be ready for death somehow when it comes; second, that in the meantime, I can live, finding true joy in all that entails. Indeed, I hope I will have plenty of time to do so.
